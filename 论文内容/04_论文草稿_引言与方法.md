## I. INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is one of the most common chronic neurological disorders, affecting approximately 60 million individuals worldwide [1]. A seizure originates from abnormal discharges in a localized group of brain regions and subsequently propagates to surrounding areas, impairing their normal function [2]. Pharmacological treatment fails in roughly 30–40% of patients, who must then rely on surgical resection or laser ablation of the epileptogenic tissue. The surgical outcome depends critically on the accurate presurgical identification of the brain regions that initiate seizures, i.e., the seizure onset zone (SOZ) [3]. At present, this identification mainly relies on expert visual inspection of intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG), which is time-consuming, subjective, and prone to human error. The nonlinearity and nonstationarity of ictal signals, together with the possibility that normal brain areas produce discharge patterns similar to those of the SOZ, further increase the difficulty of localization.

Intracranial EEG records neural activity with high spatial resolution and a high signal-to-noise ratio, and has therefore become the main measurement for presurgical evaluation [4]. Beyond inspecting individual channels, a large body of work characterizes epileptic networks through connectivity analysis, among which effective connectivity describes the directed influence that one brain region exerts over another [6]–[8]. Granger causality (GC) provides an operational definition of such directed influence: if the past of channel $j$ improves the prediction of the present of channel $i$, then $j$ is said to be Granger-causal for $i$. Classical GC analysis builds on linear vector autoregression (VAR) [12]. Because ictal iEEG exhibits strong nonlinearity, Tank et al. [12] introduced neural network-based GC estimators, including the component-wise multilayer perceptron (cMLP), in which each output channel is predicted by an individual network that takes the history of all channels as input. To make the estimated influence directly interpretable, the group lasso [13] is applied to the first-layer weights, so that each directed source–target relationship is summarized by the norm of one weight group, and connections without predictive contribution are removed entirely.

The interaction structure among channels is not static: the network reorganizes before and during a seizure, and these changes carry important information about where the seizure starts and how it propagates [TBD]. To capture this dynamics, the standard practice segments the recording into short sliding windows and estimates a separate connectivity network within each window. This estimation scheme has an inherent limitation. A window that is short enough to resolve temporal changes provides only a limited number of samples, so each per-window model must be trained on scarce data; the resulting causal matrices are affected by measurement noise, parameter initialization, and training stochasticity. Consequently, adjacent windows with nearly identical physiological content can yield noticeably different networks, and these data-independent fluctuations contaminate the estimated network evolution. They obscure genuine changes around seizure onset, complicate change-point analysis, and reduce the stability of channel rankings that SOZ localization relies on. This limitation applies to linear VAR estimators and to sparsity-regularized neural estimators alike, since both are typically trained within each window independently.

In this paper, we propose the Temporally Coupled component-wise MLP (TC-cMLP) to estimate time-varying nonlinear Granger causality from multichannel iEEG. TC-cMLP is built on the following observation: consecutive windows of the same recording share most of their nonlinear predictive structure, whereas the participating channels and their influence strengths are what evolve over time. TC-cMLP therefore assigns each window its own first layer, which encodes the causal structure of that window, and shares all subsequent layers across windows, which learn the nonlinear input–output mapping from the pooled samples of all windows. Two regularizations complete the model: group lasso on the window-specific first layers selects sparse causal connections, and a total variation penalty on the sequence of causal scores suppresses window-to-window fluctuations that lack data support while preserving changes with sufficient evidence, such as those near seizure onset.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

1. We propose TC-cMLP, a cMLP variant for dynamic nonlinear Granger causality learning that couples consecutive time windows through shared subsequent layers and window-specific first layers, together with a joint training algorithm that combines gradient updates for smooth terms with proximal updates for group sparsity.
2. We introduce a temporal regularization on the estimated causal scores that enforces continuity of the dynamic network without forbidding abrupt, data-supported changes.
3. We validate the method on synthetic data with known dynamic causal structure, showing improved edge detection, causal strength recovery, and change-point recovery compared with independently trained cMLP.
4. We apply the method to the HUP iEEG dataset and show that the dynamic causal outflow localizes the SOZ with higher stability and accuracy than per-window estimation.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II presents the proposed method. Section III reports experiments on synthetic data and HUP iEEG. Section IV concludes the paper.

## II. METHOD

The overall framework of the proposed approach is shown in Fig. 1. A multichannel iEEG recording is first divided into overlapping windows. TC-cMLP assigns each window a dedicated first layer and shares the subsequent layers across all windows, and is trained jointly on all windows with group sparsity and temporal regularization. The first-layer weights of each window yield a causal matrix, and the sequence of matrices describes the evolution of the effective connectivity network. Channel-level outflow features are then computed from these matrices to localize the SOZ.

### A. Nonlinear Granger Causality Modeling via cMLP

Let $x_t = [x_t^1, x_t^2, \ldots, x_t^p] \in \mathbb{R}^p$ denote the observation of $p$ iEEG channels at time $t$. The linear VAR model writes the present value as a weighted sum of past values,

$$
x_t = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \Phi_k x_{t-k} + \epsilon_t, \tag{1}
$$

where $K$ is the maximum lag, $\Phi_k \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ collects the lag-$k$ coefficients, and $\epsilon_t$ is zero-mean noise. If the coefficients linking channel $j$ to channel $i$ vanish at all lags, $j$ is considered Granger non-causal for $i$. Since ictal iEEG is strongly nonlinear and nonstationary, we replace the linear predictor with a nonlinear one,

$$
x_t^i = f_i(x_{t-1}, x_{t-2}, \ldots, x_{t-K}) + \epsilon_t^i, \tag{2}
$$

where each target channel $i$ is modeled by an individual network $f_i$ that receives the recent history of all channels. This component-wise design underlies the cMLP [12].

For target channel $i$, the lagged samples are arranged into the input $z_t = [x_{t-1}, x_{t-2}, \ldots, x_{t-K}] \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times K}$. The first layer computes

$$
h_{t,i}^{(1)} = \sigma\!\left(W_i^{(1)} z_t + b_i^{(1)}\right), \tag{3}
$$

where $W_i^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times p \times K}$, $H$ is the number of hidden units, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is a nonlinear activation. The subsequent layers transform this representation and output the prediction

$$
\hat{x}_t^i = F_i\!\left(h_{t,i}^{(1)}; \Theta_i\right), \tag{4}
$$

where $\Theta_i$ denotes the parameters of the layers after the first. Within the first layer, the weights connected to source channel $j$ form the group

$$
W_{i \leftarrow j}^{(1)} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times K}. \tag{5}
$$

If this group is entirely zero, the prediction of channel $i$ does not depend on the history of channel $j$, and $j$ is Granger non-causal for $i$. The causal influence of $j$ on $i$ is therefore quantified by

$$
C_{i,j} = \left\| W_{i \leftarrow j}^{(1)} \right\|_F, \tag{6}
$$

and the matrix $C \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ collects the directed causal scores, with rows indexing targets and columns indexing sources. The self-history of each channel is retained for prediction, and diagonal entries are excluded from all inter-channel network features.

### B. Temporally Coupled cMLP

To describe network dynamics, the recording is segmented into $W$ consecutive (overlapping) windows $X^{(1)}, X^{(2)}, \ldots, X^{(W)}$. Applying the cMLP of Section II-A independently in each window estimates each network from the samples of that window alone. Short windows then provide few samples per model, and the resulting matrices fluctuate due to noise and training variability rather than physiological change.

TC-cMLP is based on the assumption that consecutive windows of the same recording share most of their nonlinear predictive structure, while the participating channels and their influence strengths evolve over time. Accordingly, TC-cMLP maintains one dedicated first layer per window,

$$
W_{1,i}^{(1)}, W_{2,i}^{(1)}, \ldots, W_{W,i}^{(1)}, \qquad b_{1,i}^{(1)}, b_{2,i}^{(1)}, \ldots, b_{W,i}^{(1)}, \tag{7}
$$

and shares the parameters of all subsequent layers across windows,

$$
\Theta_i = W_{2:L,i}. \tag{8}
$$

The prediction of target channel $i$ in window $w$ is then

$$
\hat{x}_t^{i,(w)} = F_i\!\left(\sigma\!\left(W_{w,i}^{(1)} z_t + b_{w,i}^{(1)}\right); \Theta_i\right). \tag{9}
$$

Under this parameterization, the window-specific first layer encodes the causal structure of the corresponding window, while the shared layers learn the nonlinear input–output mapping from the pooled samples of all windows. The shared layers thus receive $W$ times more training samples than in per-window estimation, and the causal structures of different windows are comparable because they are read out through the same nonlinear transformation. All window-specific first layers are initialized with identical values, so that any difference between the estimated $C^{(w)}$ arises from the data rather than from initialization.

### C. Training Objective

The causal matrix of window $w$ is defined elementwise as

$$
C_{i,j}^{(w)} = \left\| W_{i \leftarrow j}^{(1,w)} \right\|_F. \tag{10}
$$

TC-cMLP is trained jointly on all windows by minimizing

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pred}} + \lambda_s \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{group}} + \lambda_t \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temporal}} + \lambda_w \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{weight}}. \tag{11}
$$

The prediction term sums the squared errors over all windows, targets, and time instants $\mathcal{T}_w$ of window $w$,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pred}} = \sum_{w=1}^{W} \sum_{i=1}^{p} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}_w} \left( x_t^{i,(w)} - \hat{x}_t^{i,(w)} \right)^2. \tag{12}
$$

The group sparsity term applies the group lasso [13] to every first-layer source–target group of every window,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{group}} = \sum_{w=1}^{W} \sum_{i=1}^{p} \sum_{j=1}^{p} \left\| W_{i \leftarrow j}^{(1,w)} \right\|_F, \tag{13}
$$

which drives entire groups to zero and produces sparse causal networks. The temporal regularization penalizes the total variation of the causal scores between adjacent windows,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temporal}} = \sum_{w=2}^{W} \left\| C^{(w)} - C^{(w-1)} \right\|_1, \tag{14}
$$

which reduces window-to-window fluctuations that lack data support while allowing a small number of pronounced, data-supported changes such as those near seizure onset. Finally, the weight decay term regularizes the shared layers,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{weight}} = \sum_{i=1}^{p} \sum_{l=2}^{L} \left\| W_{l,i} \right\|_F^2. \tag{15}
$$

The hyperparameter $\lambda_s$ controls the sparsity of each network, $\lambda_t$ controls the temporal continuity, and $\lambda_w$ controls the scale of the shared parameters. All hyperparameters are selected on synthetic data and on training/validation patients, and are then fixed for all test patients.

### D. Optimization

The objective (11) combines smooth terms with the non-smooth group lasso. Following the proximal gradient scheme of [12], each iteration consists of two steps. First, a gradient step is taken on the smooth part $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pred}} + \lambda_w \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{weight}} + \lambda_t \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{temporal}}$. Every window-specific first layer is updated with the step size $\eta$ using the gradients from its own window, whereas each shared layer aggregates gradients from all windows and is updated with the step size $\eta / W$, so that the shared layers do not require a window-count-dependent rescaling of the learning rate. Second, the proximal operator of the group lasso is applied independently to every first-layer group,

$$
W_{i \leftarrow j}^{(1,w)} \leftarrow \operatorname{prox}_{\lambda_s \eta \|\cdot\|_F}\!\left( W_{i \leftarrow j}^{(1,w)} \right)
= \max\!\left(1 - \frac{\lambda_s \eta}{\left\| W_{i \leftarrow j}^{(1,w)} \right\|_F},\, 0\right) W_{i \leftarrow j}^{(1,w)}, \tag{16}
$$

which shrinks entire groups and yields exactly zero weights for connections without predictive contribution.

During training, the full objective (11) is monitored at fixed intervals. Training stops when the monitored objective fails to improve for a prescribed number of consecutive checks, and the parameters achieving the lowest objective are restored as the final model. The training procedure checks for non-finite losses, and the estimated causal matrices are recorded as both continuous scores and binary decisions, where a connection is declared present when its group norm exceeds a threshold.

### E. Dynamic Network Features and SOZ Localization

For window $w$, the element $C_{i,j}^{(w)}$ quantifies the directed influence from source channel $j$ to target channel $i$. For each channel $j$, the outflow, inflow, and net flow are defined as

$$
\operatorname{outflow}_j^{(w)} = \sum_{i \neq j} C_{i,j}^{(w)}, \qquad
\operatorname{inflow}_j^{(w)} = \sum_{i \neq j} C_{j,i}^{(w)}, \qquad
\operatorname{netflow}_j^{(w)} = \operatorname{outflow}_j^{(w)} - \operatorname{inflow}_j^{(w)}. \tag{17}
$$

Outflow measures the causal influence that channel $j$ exerts on the rest of the network, inflow measures the influence it receives, and net flow is their difference. Based on the expectation that SOZ channels act as drivers of the ictal network, the outflow is used as the primary feature for localization, while inflow and net flow serve as supplementary analyses.

SOZ localization proceeds within each patient. In every window, channels are ranked by outflow and the top $K\%$ are marked as anomalous for that window. Restricting the statistics to ictal windows, each channel is characterized by the number of windows in which it is anomalous. Channels are then ranked by this count, and the top $K\%$ are declared as the predicted SOZ channels, with $K = 18$ in all experiments. This rule filters out sporadic fluctuations and grounds the decision on sustained network involvement across the seizure. The same rule is applied unchanged to all patients and all compared methods.
